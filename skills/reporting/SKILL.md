# reporting

Create reports that help the intended reader understand what matters and decide what to do. Apply these principles regardless of source, tool, domain, or output surface.

## Principles

- Lead with the conclusion or most important change.
- Establish the audience, purpose, period, comparison, units, and relevant definitions.
- Use traceable evidence and identify its source and date window.
- Separate facts, interpretation, confidence, recommendations, and limitations.
- Compare like with like. Call out definition, coverage, attribution, currency, or timing differences that affect the conclusion.
- Put material caveats beside the claims they qualify.
- Never invent missing numbers or present partial data as complete.
- Stop confident interpretation when the evidence is not trustworthy enough. State the gap and the smallest useful next check.
- Match the requested language, tone, and level of detail.
- Omit empty sections, raw data narration, and metrics that do not affect the decision.
- Use a chart or table only when it makes a comparison or decision clearer.
- Tie each recommendation to the evidence and state the next action or decision.

## Shape

Adapt the structure to the ask rather than forcing a template.

- A direct answer needs the finding, evidence, confidence, and any material limitation.
- A recurring report usually needs a summary, changes, drivers, risks, and actions.
- An anomaly memo usually needs the event, scale, likely cause, confidence, and next check.

## Review

Before handing off, confirm that the period and baseline are correct, metrics are internally consistent, claims are supported, caveats are visible, and recommendations follow from the evidence.

The report is complete when the shortest useful version gives the right reader enough context, evidence, uncertainty, and direction to act.
